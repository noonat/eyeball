The shell, the type scale, the palette, and every primitive the other tearouts reuse. Both themes side by side. Frames are 390 × 800, a real phone viewport rather than a shrunken preview, so proportion is judged at true scale.

Palette tokens

Ink on paper, and one highlighter. The accent is the ink itself, so a link and a button read as ink, and --hl is the only color that asks for attention. Two tones per semantic hue. The plain tone reads as text, the -vivid tone fills rules and borders. One value cannot do both in light mode, because an amber dark enough to read as text is brown. Additions are teal and removals are clay, never red against green, which is the one pair some readers cannot separate at all.

  • Teal and clay separate on the blue-yellow axis, which survives both common forms of red-green color blindness. Hue is still never the only signal: a changed row carries a sign in the gutter and a tinted background.
  • The light background is a paper with a trace of green, not pure white and never cream. Reviews happen outdoors on a phone at full brightness, where white panels on a white page lose their edges. Both themes take one neutral family at one temperature.
  • Text on the highlighter is a fixed ink. --on-hl does not change with the theme, because light text on yellow cannot be read. The dark highlighter leans toward lemon, away from the amber that marks a passage showing what it replaced.
  • Every text tone holds 4.5 to 1 on the grounds it sits on, in both themes. The light Approve green is #15805a, because white on the earlier #1a9b6c was 3.53.

Type scale

Two families, each in one role. Atkinson Hyperlegible Next and its Mono set the chrome, code and addresses: the Braille Institute drew them for low-vision legibility, and they keep l, I and 1, and 0 and O, apart. Literata sets what an agent wrote and a rendered document. The fonts are 215 KB, served beside the stylesheet, so a first load shows the system faces until they arrive.

Icons lucide

Lucide, ISC licensed, drawn on a 24 unit grid with a 2 unit stroke. Each icon is inlined into app.css as a CSS mask, so currentColor paints it and it takes the color of whatever text it sits beside. Eighteen icons come to about 5.7KB of stylesheet, with no font file, no second request, and no load state. The names in icons.txt are the names on lucide.dev.

  • A mask rather than a font, and rather than inline SVG in the markup. A font would have to ship a thousand icons or add a subsetting step, and its failure mode is the worst available: a ligature icon font renders the literal word check mid-sentence wherever the shaper does not cooperate, after looking correct everywhere it was tested. Inline SVG at the use site is correct and puts eighteen blocks of path data into the markup. A mask keeps one table in the stylesheet and a readable class at the use site.
  • Presentation attributes are stripped from each SVG and declared once in the .i rule. Stroke weight is one number rather than eighteen, so making every icon heavier at small sizes is a single edit.
  • Stroke icons at 2 units read light at 11px. That is the real cost of this family against a filled one, and it is the thing to judge on the phone rather than here. The fix, if it is needed, is the stroke width in that one rule.
  • Two icons are bound to :root as well. A file’s disclosure caret and the finished setup step are drawn by generated content, which cannot carry a class, so those two masks need a variable a pseudo-element can reach.
  • The diff gutter keeps its + and − as text. Those characters sit in a monospace column and have to align with the code beside them. An icon there is a different width and breaks the grid.
  • Three icons are in the table and not yet on a screen. They cost about 900 bytes together, which is less than the interruption of regenerating the table mid-implementation.

State pills, rows

Four states, each with a pill. Work waiting on the reviewer is the one card on the screen and its title is marked with the highlighter, which reads at arm's length as the stripe it replaced did. Working and settled rows are lines between hairlines. A waiting row carries the first paragraph of its note, clamped to two lines. The row below shows the awkward content on purpose: a long title that wraps, and an agent name long enough to need truncating.

Attention the eye, the highlighter, the letter

The eye is in the top bar of every screen: open with the waiting count as its iris while anything waits, shut when nothing does, and large and shut as the empty queue. The highlighter marks a waiting title and a line chosen for a comment. The agent's note is a letter, signed, and the reviewer's verdict note stays a plain note.

  • The mark follows the words, not the row. A title that wraps is marked as two strokes, as a pen would, which a filled card cannot do.
  • A chosen line is a lighter fill than a marked title, so the code on it still reads.
  • Two digits fit the iris. A queue of a hundred is not a case this surface is drawn for.
  • The eye is drawn in app.css rather than listed in icons.txt. Lucide has no shut eye with lashes, and the open one has to hold a count.

The diff unified

Unified, never side by side. Two columns of code on a 390px screen leaves about twenty characters each, which is not reading. The code block owns its horizontal overflow so the page never scrolls sideways and takes the decision bar with it. Word marks appear only where a run of removals pairs one to one with the additions replacing it.

  • The line-number column is hidden under 720px and returns above it. Four digits is a tenth of the readable width on a phone, and the reviewer never has to read one: a comment carries its own line range to the agent.
  • Five syntax roles, not twenty. Keyword, string, comment, number, name. More colors than that at 13px on a phone reads as noise, and the reviewer is looking for what changed rather than for a syntax error.
  • A collapsed file still shows its counts, which is what makes a file list scannable when a change is too big to open expanded.

Rendered prose markdown

Markdown opens as a document, because a patch of a paragraph is a poor way to read a paragraph. On the review page a document’s card shows what changed and folds the rest. A changed passage gets a rule beside it, and its changed words are marked where they stand, so the old and the new text are read in one place rather than compared by eye.

  • A teal rule marks a changed passage. It finds a change while scrolling, and it is the only mark on a change to formatting alone, which changes no word.
  • Changed words are marked where they stand, in a plain sentence, inside a link and inside a code span. An added word is tinted with a bar under it, and a removed one is tinted and struck through. The strike is the signal that survives without hue, and the bar marks an addition with no removal beside it. The tints are the code diff’s word marks. A removed word and the word that replaced it are drawn two pixels apart, since they touch in the source and drawn touching they read as one word.
  • A fold is a details element, so it opens with no script. Its line spans the card like a hunk header, says how many passages it holds, and turns its chevron when open. A fold at either end of the card meets the card’s edge.
  • What a passage replaced is struck through above it when words cannot be marked: a run replaced by a different number of passages, or a passage whose structure changed. Each new passage in the run is tinted whole. The struck text is smaller and in the removal color, so the new text stays the thing being read. A removed passage holds only its struck text, with the rule beside it.
  • A ticked task box marks the box, since the box is the only thing that changed.
  • Each list item is a passage, written as a list of one. Two in a row are spaced as two items of one list, so the tight task list and the loose numbered list here read as the lists they are, and an ordered item keeps its own number.
  • A code block and a table scroll inside the passage, as the diff does, so a long line never takes the page sideways. The code block uses the diff’s five syntax roles.
  • A table aligns its cells with the align attribute. The renderer’s default is an inline style, which the policy blocks.
  • A task list’s boxes are disabled. They show what is done, and nothing on this surface edits a document.
  • Nothing is marked when the base is empty. A document written from scratch is entirely new, and marking every passage marks none of them.

Comments four levels

A comment attaches to a line or range, to a passage, to a file, or to the review itself. The review level is the one with nowhere else to go, and it carries the most important comment anybody writes, so it is never behind a menu. Whatever the reviewer selected, the agent receives a path, a line range, and the quoted text.

  • A comment that failed to save says so and keeps its text. The server is what makes a comment durable, so until it answers, the surface holds what was typed rather than claiming to have stored it. Retry and delete are both on the comment, because a failed save is either worth another attempt or was not worth keeping.
  • Editing and deleting stay available until the verdict. Nothing has been released, so a point that turned out wrong by the end of the reading is removed rather than explained away.

Actions buttons, bar, notices

The decision bar is pinned to the bottom of a review, because approving a long document must not require scrolling to the end of it. Three of its states are here: nothing said yet, comments waiting to go, and the round already decided. Refusing to request changes with nothing to act on is a notice, not a disabled button with no explanation.

  • Approve is the filled button and sits on the right, under the thumb. Request changes is outlined rather than filled: it is the common verdict, and two filled buttons of equal weight make the reviewer read both before acting.
  • A notice is for what could not have been prevented. It is never a refusal of something the screen offered and then declined, which is why the warn notice above appears beside the bar rather than replacing it.

Losing the connection stated, never implied

The surface does not work offline and it does have to say when it is offline. A server-rendered page whose server is gone answers a tap by doing nothing, and the control looks identical before and after, so the reviewer taps again, holds, and concludes they missed. Nothing separates an unreachable server from a mis-aimed thumb.

  • The notice sits under the top bar and stays for as long as the condition does. It is amber rather than red: nothing has been lost, and what is already on screen is still worth reading.
  • Nothing appears while the connection is healthy. A permanent indicator is furniture within a day and stops being read, which leaves the reviewer with no signal at the moment one matters.
  • Write controls say why they cannot be used, on themselves. Dimming a button and leaving the reason to be inferred is the same failure in a quieter voice.
  • A request that failed reports on the control that made it. The banner says the connection is gone; this says what happened to the thing that was just attempted, which is a different question.
  • The typed text is still there, on a card marked as not saved. Retry and Delete are both offered, because a failed save is either worth another attempt or was not worth keeping, and only the reviewer knows which.

First run install, permission, badge

Notifications do not arrive until the surface is installed to the home screen and granted permission. That makes it the one setup step that has to be impossible to miss rather than something found later in a settings screen. The badge on the installed icon is what answers “is anything waiting” without opening anything.

  • A lapsed subscription is a state the surface reports, not one it hides. A queue that looks empty because nothing is arriving is the failure this exists to prevent, and it is silent by nature.
